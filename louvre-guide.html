📍 Arrival Instructions

Arrive by 9:15 AM and look for a staff member holding a "9:30 AM" sign near the entrance.

Enter through the Porte des Lions entrance on the Seine side of the Denon Wing. If no staff member is present at that entrance, head into the underground Carrousel du Louvre instead — accessible via 99 Rue de Rivoli or directly from the Palais Royal – Musée du Louvre métro station (lines 1 & 7).

Once inside: pass through the security checkpoint, then take the stairs or elevator up to the 1st Floor of the Denon Wing.

1

Mona Lisa (La Joconde)

Room 711 (Salle des États)  Denon Wing · 1st Floor

Leonardo da Vinci · 1503–1519 · Oil on poplar wood · 77 × 53 cm

Directions from Entrance
1After scanning your ticket, take the stairs up to the 1st Floor, Denon Wing.
2Walk straight through the Spanish and Italian painting galleries into the long corridor known as the Grand Galerie, heading east toward the centre of the museum.
3Look for the wide entrance on your right marked Room 711 — Salle des États.
4Step inside. The Mona Lisa hangs on the large freestanding wall directly across the room from the massive Veronese painting.
Did you know? She's smaller than almost everyone expects — just 77 × 53 cm, about the size of a large tablet. She was stolen in 1911 by a Louvre employee who had hidden overnight and walked out with the painting under his coat. It was missing for two full years before being recovered in Florence.
2

The Wedding Feast at Cana

Room 711 (Salle des États)  Denon Wing · 1st Floor

Paolo Veronese · 1563 · Oil on canvas · 677 × 994 cm — the largest painting in the Louvre

Directions from Mona Lisa
🚶 0 steps — same room
1Simply turn around. The Wedding Feast at Cana fills the entire wall directly opposite the Mona Lisa. Most visitors stand with their backs to it the whole time.
Did you know? Napoleon looted this painting from the monastery of San Giorgio Maggiore in Venice. When the Allies demanded its return after Waterloo in 1815, France argued it was too fragile to move — so they sent Venice a copy instead. The original Veronese stayed in Paris. Venice still has the copy today.
3

Winged Victory of Samothrace

Daru Staircase Landing  Denon / Sully Junction · 1st Floor

Ancient Greek marble sculpture · c. 190 BC · Goddess of Victory (Nike) · 2.44 m tall

Directions from Room 711
🚶 ~3 min walk
1Exit Room 711 and turn right, heading back west through the Grand Galerie the way you came in.
2Walk the full length of the Grand Galerie until you reach the Daru staircase — a wide, dramatic staircase at the junction of the Denon and Sully wings.
3She stands at the top of the staircase on a prow-shaped pedestal, impossible to miss. She commands the entire landing.
Did you know? She has no head and no arms — yet is widely considered one of the greatest sculptures ever made. Her missing right hand was discovered in 1950 in a crate in Vienna, where it had sat unidentified for decades. Scholars still debate the position of her arms: the leading theory is that her right arm was raised in a gesture of proclamation.
4

Venus de Milo (Aphrodite of Milos)

Room 16 (Salle 16)  Sully Wing · Ground Floor

Ancient Greek marble · c. 130–100 BC · Aphrodite, goddess of love and beauty · 2.02 m tall

Directions from Winged Victory
🚶 ~4 min walk
1From the Winged Victory landing, take the Daru staircase down to the ground floor.
2At the bottom, turn left and follow the signs into the Sully Wing — Greek Antiquities.
3Continue into Room 16. Venus de Milo stands in the centre of the room on a tall pedestal — you'll see her from the doorway.
Did you know? When she was found on the island of Milos in 1820, additional fragments were also recovered — including a hand holding an apple and an inscription naming the sculptor. Those fragments mysteriously disappeared, possibly to avoid competition with the already-famous Medici Venus in Florence. Without the inscription, we still don't know for certain who made her.
5

The Seated Scribe (Le Scribe accroupi)

Room 22 — Egyptian Antiquities  Sully Wing · Ground Floor

4th Dynasty Egypt · c. 2600–2350 BC · Painted limestone, alabaster, rock crystal · 53.7 cm tall

Directions from Venus de Milo
🚶 ~3 min walk
1From Room 16, continue east through the Sully Wing ground floor, following signs for Egyptian Antiquities.
2Enter Room 22. The Seated Scribe is displayed in a glass case near the centre — small, only 54 cm tall, but instantly commanding.
Did you know? His eyes are made from rock crystal set in copper frames with alabaster surrounds — which is why they appear so disconcertingly alive after 4,500 years. Visitors regularly describe feeling that he is watching them. He was discovered at Saqqara in 1850, already holding the papyrus roll that identified him as a scribe of high rank.
6

Code of Hammurabi

Room 3 — Near Eastern Antiquities  Richelieu Wing · Ground Floor

Babylonian · 1754 BC · Black diorite basalt stele · 2.25 m tall · One of the earliest written legal codes in human history

Directions from Seated Scribe
🚶 ~5 min walk
1Exit the Egyptian Antiquities rooms and cross north through the Sully Wing toward the Richelieu Wing, following signs for Near Eastern Antiquities.
2Find Room 3. The stele stands alone in the room — a towering black column you can't miss.
Did you know? The code contains 282 laws and is famous for "an eye for an eye" — but punishments varied sharply by social class. If a nobleman knocked out another nobleman's tooth, his tooth was knocked out in return. If he knocked out a commoner's tooth, he simply paid a fine. The relief carved at the top shows King Hammurabi receiving the laws directly from Shamash, the sun god, legitimising the code as divinely ordained.
7

Michelangelo's Slaves

Room 4 — Italian Sculpture  Denon Wing · Ground Floor

Michelangelo Buonarroti · 1513–1516 · Marble · The Dying Slave (228 cm) & The Rebellious Slave (215 cm)

Directions from Hammurabi's Code
🚶 ~5 min walk
1From the Richelieu Wing, head south back toward the Denon Wing on the ground floor.
2Follow signs for Italian Sculpture, entering Room 4. The two Slaves stand prominently in the gallery — their raw, unfinished marble is immediately visible.
Did you know? They were commissioned for the never-completed tomb of Pope Julius II. Michelangelo gave them away to a Florentine banker, Roberto Strozzi, who then gifted them to King Francis I of France — which is why two Michelangelos ended up in Paris. The apparently "unfinished" quality may have been intentional: Michelangelo believed the sculpture already existed within the marble; his task was merely to liberate it.
8

Apollo Gallery & the Crown Jewels

Room 66 — Galerie d'Apollon  Denon Wing · 1st Floor

17th-century royal ceremonial gallery · French Crown Jewels including the 140-carat Régent Diamond

Directions from Michelangelo's Slaves
🚶 ~3 min walk (go up one floor)
1From Room 4, take the stairs up to the 1st floor of the Denon Wing.
2Follow signs for Galerie d'Apollon — Room 66. The gallery runs east-west and is recognisable immediately by its extraordinary gilded ceiling.
Did you know? The ceiling was commissioned by Louis XIV and designed by Charles Le Brun — it directly inspired Versailles. But it was left unfinished for nearly 200 years. The central oval was finally painted in 1851 by Eugène Delacroix, the same artist who painted Liberty Leading the People. The 140-carat Régent Diamond on display here was once set into Napoleon's sword hilt for his coronation.
9

The Raft of the Medusa

Room 700 — Large French Paintings  Denon Wing · 1st Floor

Théodore Géricault · 1818–1819 · Oil on canvas · 491 × 716 cm · French Romanticism

Directions from Apollo Gallery
🚶 ~2 min walk — same floor
1Continue east along the 1st floor Denon Wing into the Large French Paintings rooms, following signs for Room 700.
2The Raft of the Medusa covers the majority of one entire wall — you will see it as you enter the room.
Did you know? It depicts a real political scandal. The French frigate Méduse ran aground in 1816 because its captain was an incompetent government appointee. Of 147 survivors placed on a makeshift raft, only 15 survived 13 days of starvation, violence, and cannibalism. The painting caused a furore when exhibited because it implicitly blamed the government. Géricault interviewed survivors and obtained actual limbs from a Paris hospital to ensure anatomical accuracy while painting.
10

Liberty Leading the People

Room 700 — Large French Paintings  Denon Wing · 1st Floor

Eugène Delacroix · 1830 · Oil on canvas · 260 × 325 cm · Commemorates the July Revolution of 1830

Directions from Raft of the Medusa
🚶 0 steps — same room
1Look to the adjacent wall in Room 700. Liberty Leading the People hangs directly in the same gallery.
Did you know? The figure of Liberty was scandalous at the time partly because she has visible armpit hair — a deliberate statement by Delacroix that she was a real woman, not a sanitised allegory. He painted himself into the scene as the man in the top hat to her right. The painting later inspired Victor Hugo while writing Les Misérables, and influenced the design of the Statue of Liberty. Despite painting it, Delacroix was not at the barricades — he admitted he participated in no fighting whatsoever.

⏱ Time & Practical Tips

You have 3 hours (9:30 AM – 12:30 PM). At roughly 15 minutes per stop including walking, this route fits comfortably without rushing.

The museum gets significantly busier after 11 AM — prioritise the Mona Lisa first while the room is still manageable.

Toilets are available near the Pyramid (ground floor) and beside the Daru staircase (1st floor).

The café on the ground floor of the Richelieu Wing (near Hammurabi's Code) is a good mid-route stop if needed.
