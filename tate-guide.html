📍 Arrival & Orientation

Enter from the riverside (north) entrance facing the Millennium Bridge — this drops you directly into the Turbine Hall at Level 1. The south entrance (Bankside) brings you in from street level at a different floor.

Grab a free map at the information desk immediately on entry — Tate Modern's permanent collection moves periodically, so verify room locations on the day. Admission to the permanent collection is free; only special exhibitions are ticketed.

Lifts and stairs are at both ends of the Turbine Hall. Nearest station: Southwark (Jubilee line) or Blackfriars (Circle/District) — both a 5-min walk.

⚠️ Collection note: Tate Modern rotates works periodically. If a piece listed below is not in its usual location, ask staff or check the gallery map — it is likely in temporary storage or a ticketed exhibition.

The Turbine Hall & Hyundai Commission

Level 1 — Main entrance spine  Boiler House

35 m high · 152 m long · Annual commissioned installation · Always free

On Arrival
1Walk through the riverside entrance doors. You are immediately in the Turbine Hall — pause and look up. The scale alone is the first thing to absorb.
2Walk the full length of the Hall to take in the current Hyundai Commission installation before heading upstairs.
Did you know? This was a working coal-fired power station until 1981, generating enough electricity to power the entire south of London. The annual Hyundai Commission is one of the most prestigious public art commissions in the world — past works include Olafur Eliasson's artificial indoor sun (2003), which 2 million people visited in 16 weeks; Ai Weiwei's floor of 100 million handmade porcelain sunflower seeds (2010); and Carsten Höller's giant playground slides (2006).
2

Mark Rothko — Seagram Murals

Level 2 — Dedicated Rothko Room  Boiler House

Mark Rothko · 1958–59 · Oil on canvas · Nine large-scale paintings in a dedicated darkened room

Directions from Turbine Hall
🚶 ~3 min
1From the Turbine Hall, take the stairs or lift up to Level 2, Boiler House.
2Follow gallery signs to the Rothko Room — a purposely darkened room. Allow your eyes to adjust before forming any opinion of the paintings.
Did you know? Rothko was commissioned in 1958 to paint murals for the Four Seasons — New York's most expensive new restaurant. He accepted a huge fee, painted these brooding dark canvases, then had lunch in the restaurant. He was so appalled by the "atmosphere of those who are at the top of the social heap" that he cancelled the commission, returned the fee in full, and kept every painting. He donated them to the Tate days before his death by suicide in 1970. Several arrived damaged when the shipping container leaked during transit.
3

Roy Lichtenstein — Whaam!

Level 2 — Boiler House galleries  Boiler House

Roy Lichtenstein · 1963 · Acrylic and oil on canvas · 172 × 406 cm · Two-panel painting

Directions from Rothko Room
🚶 ~2 min — same level
1Exit the Rothko Room and continue through the Level 2 galleries, checking the free map for the current location of Whaam! — it is typically nearby in the same floor's pop art section.
Did you know? Lichtenstein copied this almost exactly from a single panel in a 1962 DC Comics war comic (All-American Men of War #89). The original illustrator was Irv Novick, who was simultaneously flattered and unsettled by Lichtenstein's international fame from it. Tate bought Whaam! in 1966 for £3,940. At nearly four metres wide — the size of four doors — it was one of Lichtenstein's largest works. It would be worth hundreds of millions today.
4

Salvador Dalí — Metamorphosis of Narcissus

Level 2 — Surrealism galleries  Boiler House

Salvador Dalí · 1937 · Oil on canvas · 51.1 × 78.1 cm · Surrealism

Directions from Whaam!
🚶 ~2 min — same level
1Continue through the Level 2 galleries following signs for Surrealism. The Dalí is typically displayed in the same general area. Check your map.
Did you know? This is the only painting Dalí ever accompanied with a poem he wrote himself — the only known case of him producing verse to interpret his own work. The double image at the heart of it (the kneeling Narcissus figure on the left becomes a stone hand holding an egg on the right, from which a narcissus flower blooms) was painted using his "paranoiac-critical method" — training himself to see double images at will. He sent a postcard of the painting to Sigmund Freud to provoke a reaction. Freud replied but was not impressed.
5

Pablo Picasso — The Three Dancers

Level 2 — Cubism / Modern European  Boiler House

Pablo Picasso · 1925 · Oil on canvas · 215 × 142 cm

Directions from Dalí
🚶 ~2 min — same level
1Remain on Level 2 and continue into the Cubism and Modern European galleries — check the map for the Three Dancers' current position.
Did you know? Picasso painted this immediately after learning of the death of his close friend Ramon Pichot. The violent, distorted central figure — arms splayed wide, head thrown back — is widely read as a crucifix, grief made physical. He refused to sell it for four decades, considering it too personal to part with. When he finally agreed to sell it to the Tate in 1965, he said: "I wonder if I should have kept it." It marked a sharp emotional break from his earlier analytical Cubism.
6

Henri Matisse — The Snail

Level 5 — Boiler House  Boiler House

Henri Matisse · 1953 · Gouache on paper, cut and pasted on paper · 286.4 × 287 cm

Directions from Three Dancers
🚶 ~4 min — go up to Level 5
1Take the stairs or lift from Level 2 up to Level 5, Boiler House.
2Follow gallery signs — The Snail is a large (nearly 3 × 3 m) work and tends to dominate whatever room it occupies. Check the map for current placement.
Did you know? Matisse made The Snail at age 84, confined to bed and unable to stand at an easel. He worked with scissors and pre-painted sheets of paper — what he called "painting with scissors" — directing assistants to pin coloured shapes to the walls of his Nice hotel room. The spiral arrangement of colour blocks is barely recognisable as a snail, but he was precise about the geometry. He died the following year. Tate acquired it in 1962 and it remains almost impossible to photograph — no reproduction captures the radiance of the hand-painted gouache colours in person.
7

Andy Warhol

Blavatnik Building galleries  Blavatnik

Andy Warhol · Various works from the 1960s–80s · Silkscreen and acrylic on canvas

Directions from The Snail
🚶 ~5 min — cross to Blavatnik Building
1Descend from Level 5 back to Level 1 or 2 and cross the Turbine Hall into the Blavatnik Building (the newer building on the south side).
2Check the free map for the current Warhol gallery location within the Blavatnik Building.
Did you know? Warhol began silk-screening celebrity faces in 1962 after noticing how newspaper photo-reproduction degraded and repeated images — he was interested in the flattening effect of mass media on identity. He deliberately ran his New York studio as a production line and called it "The Factory." In 1968 he was shot by Valerie Solanas and survived, but remained in chronic pain for the rest of his life — a fact he rarely discussed publicly. He wore a surgical corset every day until his death in 1987.
8

Joseph Beuys — The Pack (Das Rudel)

Blavatnik Building  Blavatnik

Joseph Beuys · 1969 · 24 wooden sleds, felt, fat, flashlights, emerging from a VW bus

Directions from Warhol
🚶 ~3 min — same building
1Continue through the Blavatnik Building galleries — check the map for The Pack's current location.
Did you know? Beuys claimed he was shot down over Crimea in WWII and kept alive by Tartar nomads who wrapped him in felt and animal fat for warmth. The felt, fat, and sleds in The Pack refer to this survival myth — felt as insulation, fat as energy, sleds as escape. Historians have found little evidence the story is literally true, but Beuys maintained it as his personal autobiography for the rest of his life. Whether or not it happened, the myth shaped one of the 20th century's most distinctive bodies of work.
9

Claude Monet — Water Lilies

Boiler House galleries  Boiler House

Claude Monet · c. 1906 · Oil on canvas · From the Nymphéas series of c. 250 paintings

Directions from The Pack
🚶 ~4 min — back across to Boiler House
1Cross back through the Turbine Hall into the Boiler House and check the map for the current location of the Monet Water Lilies.
Did you know? You visited Giverny just one week ago — Monet's actual garden pond is the subject of this painting. He began the Water Lilies series in 1896 and painted approximately 250 versions over 30 years, working increasingly from memory as cataracts progressively blinded him. The condition shifted his palette toward the warmer, more abstract canvases of his later years. After cataract surgery in 1923 he reportedly repainted sections of earlier works that now disappointed him — seeing, for the first time, what he had actually put down.

Louise Bourgeois — Maman

Exterior — Southwest entrance, Bankside  Outside

Louise Bourgeois · 1999 · Bronze, stainless steel, marble · 9.27 m tall · Permanent installation

Directions from inside the museum
🚶 ~3 min — exit to Bankside
1Exit the museum via the south (Bankside) entrance, or walk around the building to the riverside terrace. Maman stands outside the southwest corner — a towering bronze spider visible from a distance.
2Walk underneath her. Look up into the egg sac — it contains 26 white marble eggs.
Did you know? Bourgeois made the first Maman for Tate Modern's original Turbine Hall commission when the museum opened in 2000. The spider represented her mother, a tapestry weaver and restorer. She said: "Like a spider, my mother was a weaver. My best friend was my mother and she was deliberate, clever, patient, soothing, reasonable, dainty, subtle, indispensable, neat and as useful as a spider." Bourgeois made her most celebrated works after the age of 70. She continued working until her death at age 98 in 2010.

⏱ Time & Practical Tips

You have 2 hours (10:00 AM – 12:00 PM). At roughly 10–12 minutes per stop, the route above is tight but achievable. If you want breathing room, drop stops 7 or 8 and go deeper on the others.

The Level 6 restaurant has excellent Thames and St Paul's views — worth a coffee stop if time permits.

The riverside walkway from Tate Modern to Southwark (for Donabe, your 12:30 PM lunch) is a pleasant 8-min walk east along the Thames.
