📍 Arrival & Orientation

Enter from Trafalgar Square via the main portico entrance on the north side. You will arrive in the Central Vestibule, from which all four wings are accessible.

Admission is free. Grab a free floor plan at the information desk — room numbers are printed on the map and marked above each doorway.

⚠️ Closing time: The gallery closes at 6:00 PM on Thursdays (open until 9 PM on Fridays only). You have approximately 1 hour 15 minutes. The route below is prioritised accordingly — see the Priority 5 box at the top.

Nearest station: Charing Cross (5 min walk) or Leicester Square (5 min walk).

⏱ Time note: With 1h15min, focus on stops 1–5 at minimum (Sainsbury Wing + West Wing). Stops 6–10 are the North and East Wings — attempt them if you have energy and time after the first five.

1

Jan van Eyck — The Arnolfini Portrait

Room 56  Sainsbury Wing

Jan van Eyck · 1434 · Oil on oak panel · 82.2 × 60 cm · Early Netherlandish painting

From Main Entrance
🚶 ~4 min
1From the Central Vestibule, turn left (west) and walk through to the Sainsbury Wing — a 1991 addition accessible via a connecting corridor.
2Ascend the stairs to the upper level and navigate to Room 56. The Arnolfini Portrait is a relatively small panel painting — look for a crowd gathered around a modest frame.
Did you know? Look at the convex mirror on the back wall — it reflects the entire room, including two figures in the doorway, one of whom is believed to be Van Eyck himself. Above the mirror, he inscribed in elaborate gothic script: "Johannes de Eyck fuit hic" — "Jan van Eyck was here" — one of the earliest artist signatures in Western art. The painting was bought by the National Gallery in 1842 for £600 from a financially distressed British army officer.
2

Leonardo da Vinci — The Virgin of the Rocks

Room 66  Sainsbury Wing

Leonardo da Vinci · c. 1491–1508 · Oil on poplar panel · 189.5 × 120 cm

Directions from Room 56
🚶 ~2 min — same wing
1Continue through the Sainsbury Wing to Room 66. The painting occupies a prominent position on one of the main walls.
Did you know? This is the second version of this composition Leonardo painted. The first (c.1483–85) is in the Louvre — which means if you were paying attention in Paris, you've now seen both. The Milanese confraternity who commissioned the original rejected it for reasons still debated; Leonardo then produced this version over roughly 15 years with workshop assistance. The two differ subtly: this version adds halos and a pointing angel, and the lighting is slightly different. Scholars still argue which Leonardo preferred.
3

Sandro Botticelli — Venus and Mars

Room 58  Sainsbury Wing

Sandro Botticelli · c. 1483–85 · Tempera and oil on poplar panel · 69.2 × 173.4 cm

Directions from Room 66
🚶 ~2 min — same wing
1Navigate back through the Sainsbury Wing to Room 58. The painting is unusually wide and horizontal — painted for a piece of furniture, likely a spalliera (headboard or chest panel).
Did you know? The wasps nesting in Mars's armour and buzzing around his head are almost certainly a punning reference to the Vespucci family — vespucci means "little wasps" in Italian — who are believed to have commissioned the painting as a wedding gift. The allegory is pointed: Venus (Love) watches alert and composed while Mars (War) sleeps, exhausted and defeated. One of the baby satyrs blows into a conch shell directly in Mars's ear and he doesn't stir. Love wins.
4

Michelangelo — The Entombment

Room 8  West Wing

Michelangelo Buonarroti · c. 1500–01 · Oil and tempera on wood · 161.7 × 149.9 cm · Unfinished

Directions from Room 58
🚶 ~4 min — cross into West Wing
1Exit the Sainsbury Wing and cross into the West Wing of the main building, heading for Room 8.
2The Entombment is a relatively intimate panel — note the visibly unfinished lower-right figure, barely sketched in.
Did you know? This is one of only four surviving panel paintings by Michelangelo — primarily a sculptor and fresco painter, he worked in this medium rarely. The figure at lower right was never completed and the identity of the commissioner remains unknown. The painting was acquired by the National Gallery in 1868 for £2,000, at the time attributed to a minor student. The Michelangelo attribution was only confirmed after purchase — the Gallery had accidentally bought a Michelangelo for the price of a studio piece.
5

Raphael — Pope Julius II

Room 8  West Wing

Raphael · 1511–12 · Oil on poplar · 108.7 × 81 cm · Portrait of the warrior pope

Directions from The Entombment
🚶 0 steps — same room
1Look around Room 8. The Pope Julius II portrait hangs in the same gallery — an intimate, psychologically charged depiction of an old man lost in thought.
Did you know? When this portrait was publicly displayed in Rome in 1513, witnesses reported that viewers "trembled as if it were the living man." Raphael essentially invented the modern psychological portrait with this work — capturing not just the sitter's appearance but an inner state (Julius appears tired, reflective, perhaps melancholy near the end of his turbulent reign). There are three versions of this portrait; this is considered the prime autograph version. Julius was Michelangelo's patron for the Sistine Chapel — he and Raphael overlapped in Rome at the same moment.
6

Diego Velázquez — The Rokeby Venus

Room 30  North Wing

Diego Velázquez · c. 1647–51 · Oil on canvas · 122.5 × 177 cm · The only surviving nude by Velázquez

Directions from Room 8
🚶 ~5 min — cross to North Wing
1Exit the West Wing and cross through the Central Vestibule into the North Wing, navigating to Room 30.
Did you know? This is the only surviving nude by Velázquez — and one of the very few female nudes in all of Spanish Golden Age painting, at a time when the Inquisition actively policed such imagery. It survived by being listed discreetly in private inventories. In March 1914, suffragette Mary Richardson walked into the National Gallery and slashed the painting seven times with a meat cleaver, declaring she was "destroying the most beautiful woman in mythological history as a protest against the government for destroying Mrs. Pankhurst." The canvas was successfully restored and all seven slashes remain faintly visible if you look closely.
7

Rembrandt — Self Portrait at the Age of 63

Room 23  North Wing

Rembrandt van Rijn · 1669 · Oil on canvas · 86 × 70.5 cm · His final self-portrait

Directions from Room 30
🚶 ~3 min — same wing
1Continue through the North Wing to Room 23. The self-portrait is a modestly sized canvas — approach close enough to see the paint texture.
Did you know? Painted in the year of his death, this was almost certainly Rembrandt's last self-portrait. He painted over 40 self-portraits through his life — more than any artist before him — creating an unparalleled visual autobiography from confident young master to bankrupt, bereft old man. He died within months of completing this one. The laughter lines, sagging jowls, and clouded eyes are rendered without mercy, vanity, or self-pity. Many art historians consider it the most honest self-portrait ever made.
8

J.M.W. Turner — The Fighting Temeraire

Room 34  East Wing

J.M.W. Turner · 1839 · Oil on canvas · 90.7 × 121.6 cm · Voted the greatest painting in Britain

Directions from Room 23
🚶 ~5 min — cross to East Wing
1Cross the Central Vestibule and enter the East Wing, navigating to Room 34.
Did you know? Voted the greatest painting in Britain in a 2005 BBC public poll. The Temeraire was a 98-gun warship that had fought heroically at the Battle of Trafalgar in 1805; Turner witnessed her being towed to a Rotherhithe breaker's yard and was moved to paint the scene as a meditation on the death of the age of sail. Despite being one of the National Gallery's most beloved works, Turner refused to sell it, calling it "my darling." He left his entire collection to the nation upon his death in 1851 — the Gallery received 300 oil paintings and nearly 30,000 works on paper.
9

Georges Seurat — Bathers at Asnières

Room 43  East Wing

Georges Seurat · 1884 · Oil on canvas · 201 × 300 cm · Post-Impressionism

Directions from Room 34
🚶 ~2 min — same wing
1Continue east through the East Wing to Room 43. Bathers at Asnières fills most of one wall — look for the large horizontal canvas of figures by the Seine.
Did you know? Seurat was just 24 when he completed this, and it was rejected by the official Paris Salon. Rather than the pointillist dot technique he later became famous for, he painted most of this in conventional brushstrokes — then went back over it and applied dots of contrasting colour on top in places, making it a hybrid of two methods simultaneously in development. The factory chimneys of Clichy smoking in the background are a deliberate statement: this is a modern working-class scene, not a classical idyll.
10

Vincent van Gogh — Sunflowers

Room 45  East Wing

Vincent van Gogh · 1888 · Oil on canvas · 92.1 × 73 cm · One of five versions Van Gogh painted

Directions from Room 43
🚶 ~1 min — adjacent room
1Walk one room further east into Room 45. The Sunflowers canvas glows from across the room — the yellow is immediately arresting.
Did you know? Van Gogh painted five versions of sunflowers in vases in Arles in 1888–89, intending them to decorate Paul Gauguin's room in the Yellow House as a welcome gift. After their famous falling out and the ear incident, Gauguin asked Van Gogh to give him one of the sunflower paintings. Van Gogh refused. In 1987, a Japanese insurance company paid £24.75 million at auction for one version — then a world record for any painting. This National Gallery version is widely considered the finest of the five. Van Gogh painted it in a single session.

⏱ Practical Tips

Closes 6:00 PM on Thursdays. If time is tight, prioritise stops 1–5 (Sainsbury + West Wings) and then jump directly to stop 8 (Turner) in the East Wing — those four wings each take about 15 minutes to walk between.

The gallery café is in the basement beneath the main building — good for a quick espresso on arrival.

Trafalgar Square is directly outside the main entrance — worth a moment on the steps before heading to the BBC Proms.
